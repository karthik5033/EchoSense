PROJECT SPECIFICATION & SYNOPSIS
Title of Project:
TopoAE: A Topological Machine Learning Framework for Structural Damage Precursor Detection from Smartphone Acoustic Emission Signals
Abstract
Structural health monitoring (SHM) of civil and mechanical infrastructures is of paramount importance to avoid catastrophic failure and assure operational safety. Conventional acoustic emission (AE) methods are capable of detecting early fracture processes with high sensitivity; however, they require costly and specialized ultrasonic transducers and high-speed data acquisition systems which drastically limits continuous and large-scale deployment. While commodity smartphone devices represent a low-cost and accessible edge-sensing solution, their built-in acoustic sensors have sensitivities in the audible bands and are highly sensitive to environmental amplitudes and noise.
In this work, we propose TopoAE, a topological machine learning framework for smartphone-recorded AE signals aimed at structural damage precursor detection. We first reconstruct the underlying dynamical state-space of continuous acoustic time-series using Takens delay-coordinate embedding, followed by persistent homology-based topological data analysis to encode multiscale topological summaries as intrinsic geometric invariants of the signal, robust to AE measurement noise and monotonic distortions. We vectorize the topological persistence diagrams through persistence representations and train an unsupervised one-dimensional convolutional autoencoder (Conv1D-AE) on baseline undamaged structural responses. Damage precursor events are detected before macroscopic failure through statistical shifts of topological features in the latent space and change-point detection of signal reconstruction residuals. We evaluate TopoAE on benchmark acoustic datasets under different loading and boundary conditions. Our framework offers an objective foundation for accessible edge-based SHM solutions by enabling large-scale and noise-resilient AE monitoring of physical infrastructures while establishing a practical link between computational topology and operational safety.

Introduction
Background
Structural Health Monitoring (in short, SHM) refers to the process of measuring responses to derive information on changes occurring in a structure’s condition. Acoustic emission (AE) is a well-known non-destructive monitoring method, where AE signals linked to stress-related wave propagation are analyzed to derive information on processes occurring within the structure. Several recent studies have demonstrated the potential of machine learning in the analysis of AE data. Specifically, Barbosh et al. demonstrated the use of time-domain waveforms and a deep-learning model to predict structural damage-severity and approximate-location [1]. In another study, Shi et al. demonstrated fatigue-damage classification using amplitude-, energy-, duration- and frequency-based features extracted from the AE signals, and employing machine learning classifiers [2]. Additionally, it has been shown that employing deep-learning segmentation models can enable automated identification of AE event onsets [4].
Smartphones have been utilized as an alternative for affordable sensing nodes for SHM. Figueiredo et al. presented the concept of bridge monitoring using a smartphone’s accelerometer [5], while in Sun’s study, wall condition was assessed using a vibration excitations, smartphone inertial sensors, and machine learning classifiers [6]. In a recent survey, Zhang et al. identified smartphones as a promising SHM platform, noting environmental disturbances, hardware variations, and other considerations as major challenges [7]. Smartphone microphones have also been utilized in structural acoustics; in the study by Sinthaworn et al., smartphone-recorded hammer-sounding impacts were classified to detect concrete delamination [8]. However, it should be noted that such an approach involves active acoustic sounding, as opposed to passive AE monitoring.
Topological methods offer another avenue for time-series analysis. Sayyad and Bazzi presented the use of Takens delay embedding and persistent homology for time-series classification [9], while Lin et al. investigated the use of persistent-homology-based feature engineering for time series prediction tasks [10].
Problem Statement
Among the capabilities presented in the literature, these have been demonstrated individually or in partial combinations, but their integration for smartphone-recorded structural acoustic signals using topological representations for damage-related classification has not been demonstrated. The applicability of temporal change detection and independent anomaly validation to this sensing configuration also needs experimental evaluation.
Motivation
A smartphone-based acoustic sensing approach might offer a readily available platform to explore structural acoustic behavior without the assumption of the capabilities of dedicated AE instrumentation. But smartphone microphones have different sensing characteristics and bandwidth limitations from conventional AE sensors [7]. Thus, TopoAE is motivated by the need to experimentally ascertain if topological representations of smartphone-recorded structural acoustic signals contain useful information for identifying damage-related events or conditions, while also evaluating the limits of the sensing modality and the analytical pipeline proposed.
Literature Survey / Review of Related Work
Acoustic Emission-Based Structural Damage Detection
Acoustic emission (AE) signals have increasingly been analyzed using machine-learning techniques in order to assess the damage of a structure. Barbosh et al. [1] proposed AE-DenseNet that directly processes time-domain AE waveforms for damage severity and approximate location prediction, demonstrating the possibility of learning structural information while bypassing conventional AE feature extraction. Shi et al. [2] instead made use of amplitude, count, energy, duration and frequency-related descriptors via machine-learning models to classify fatigue damage stages. Hamdi et al. [3] investigated signal decomposition and machine-learning methods for identifying crack-growth mechanisms of heterogeneous structures, while Melchiorre et al. [4] used a 1-D U-Net to automate AE event-onset detection.

Smartphone-Based Structural Health Monitoring
Smartphones have also been investigated for their potential as affordable sensing platforms for structural monitoring. Figueiredo et al. [5] demonstrated bridge monitoring using smartphone accelerometer measurements while Sun [6] investigated vibration challenge-response sensing using smartphones inertial sensors and machine-learning classifiers. Finally, Zhang et al. [7] reviewed smartphone sensing for SHM identifying practical issues including environmental effects, synchronization, sensor limitations and cross-device consistency. In this context, [8] provide a closer acoustic precedent using the smartphone microphone to acquire hammer-sounding responses for concrete delamination classification. However, in this study is concerned with active hammer sounding, not passive conventional AE, and so smartphone microphones should not be considered equivalent to dedicated ultrasonic AE sensors.
Topological Time-Series Representation
Topological data-analysis methods offer an alternative representation to complex time-series signals. Sayyad and Bazzi [9] proposed TopTimeNet that uses Takens delay embedding and persistent homology to obtain geometric and topological descriptors for the purpose of time series classification. In turn, Lin et al. [10] proposed to incorporate the persistent-homology features into deep-learning architecture for time-series prediction. These works provide a methodological support for using Takens embeddings and persistent homology for constructing features, but none of them evaluate the approach on the smartphone-recorded structural acoustic signals. smartphone microphones should not be considered equivalent to dedicated ultrasonic AE sensors.
Anomaly Detection and Temporal Change Detection
SUnsupervised anomaly detection offers an alternative approach to structural monitoring where labelled damage data are scarce. Wang et al. [11] explored deep autoencoders with information fusion and active sensing for structural anomaly finding, but their measurements were made using accelerometers rather than smartphone acoustic sensors. Adams and MacKay [12] proposed Bayesian online changepoint detection of sequential information; however, the application to structural acoustic measurements has not been established in the selected literature pool. Such approaches can thus be considered as contributing methodological stages to be experimentally investigated as independent anomaly-validation and temporal-change-detection stages within TopoAE.

Dataset Description
The proposed framework will be evaluated using publicly available acoustic emission (AE) datasets that include raw AE waveforms and their associated structural-condition or event labels. The main dataset used in this study will be prestressed Reinforced-Concrete (RC) bridge-girder AE dataset reported by D’Angela and Magliulo [13]. This dataset consists of raw AE recordings from four prestressed RC girders subjected to cyclic and monotonic four-point bending tests. The tests were performed using piezoelectric AE sensors at 10 MHz sampling frequency, and focused on cracks of low-to-moderate size. The dataset provides publicly available raw AE data for analysis [13]
Figure 5.2.1. Experimental four-point loading configuration for the prestressed RC bridge girder used for acoustic emission testing, adapted from D’Angela and Magliulo [13]. 
A secondary dataset provided by Siracusano et al. [14] will be used for initial model development and comparative evaluation. The dataset consists of 15,000 training and 1,650 testing AE events, each of which contains three classes of AE events (tensile, shear, and mixed-mode) and 1,000 time samples. The raw MATLAB files publicly distributed by the authors are sampled at a reduced sampling frequency of 500 kHz [14]. Mendeley Data
The raw signals will undergo quality inspection, exploratory data analysis, normalization, noise handling, and event segmentation where required. Dataset partitions will be formed carefully to minimize data leakage, with specimen- or experiment-level separation being used where the available metadata permits.

5.3.2 Random Forest Classification
The extracted topological feature vectors are provided to a 
Random Forest (RF) classifier
 for 
classification of the experimentally defined structural acoustic conditions/events.
The RF model consists of multiple decision trees whose predictions are aggregated to obtain the 
final class prediction. The number of trees, tree depth, minimum samples per split, and 
class-weighting strategy will be determined experimentally using the collected dataset.
Random Forest is used as the primary classifier because it provides a suitable machine-learning 
model for evaluating the discriminative capability of the extracted topological features. It is 
treated as an established classification technique rather than a novel contribution.
5.3.3 Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection
To analyse temporal changes in the recorded signal or derived feature stream, 
Bayesian Online 
Changepoint Detection (BOCD)
 will be investigated. The method estimates the probability of 
a change in the underlying statistical behaviour of a sequential signal and identifies candidate 
change points.
The approach is based on the Bayesian online changepoint framework proposed by Adams and 
MacKay [12]. In TopoAE, this method will be applied to the selected sequential representation 
after experimental evaluation of the appropriate input feature/score stream.

Evaluation Metrics & Validation Plan
The performance of the proposed TopoAE framework will be benchmarked on the basis of classification, anomaly-detection, and changepoint discovery metrics. In particular, the proposed method will be compared to conventional acoustic/AE features and the CNN baseline, using identical evaluation protocols.
Classification Metrics
For the task of classification, the following performance measures will be reported:
• Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score
• Confusion matrix
• ROC-AUC, where applicable
These metrics will be calculated for the TopoAE + random forest model, compared to the conventional acoustic/AE features baseline and the CNN baseline.
Anomaly Detection Metrics
For the task of anomaly detection with the autoencoders, the following performance measures will be reported:
• Reconstruction error
• ROC-AUC
• PR-AUC, where applicable
• Confusion matrix at a selected threshold
Changepoint Detection Metrics
For the task of changepoint detection, with the Bayesian online changepoint detection algorithm, the following performance measures will be reported:
• Change-point precision/recall, where temporal ground-truth is available
• Detection delay, defined as the difference between the reference change-point and the detected change-point
Where temporal ground-truth is not available, the changepoint detection results will be reported in the exploratory analysis, rather than as a formal benchmark of damage onset detection performance
Validation Strategy:
To avoid overfitting on the level of individual specimens in the prestressed RC bridge-girder dataset, where possible, the specimens will be held out at the level of individual girders, so that training and test sets do not contain data from the same girder. 
With only four girders available in the bridge dataset, leave-one-specimen-out validation will be considered, rather than hold-out of individual signal windows.
 For the secondary concrete AE dataset, the initial training/test split will be respected, and additional grouped validation will be considered, if metadata on the specimens are available. 
The following benchmarks will be considered:
1. conventional acoustic/AE features + random forest
2. CNN baseline
3. topological features + random forest
Additionally, an ablation study will be carried out, to assess the contribution of the topological representation, by comparing the TopoAE performance to reduced sets of features. The validation procedure will be consistent across benchmarks, in terms of data splits and performance metrics.
Ethical Considerations:
This project involves analysis of data from research datasets only, and does not involve collection/redistribution of patient data. 
No sensitive information is expected to be present in the analyzed datasets. The datasets will be cited in accordance with their licenses/relevant publications.
This project will not make claims of detecting damage on the basis of phone sensors, where conventional AE datasets are used – the distinction between the two types of data will be explicitly noted, where applicable.
